# Sonar Advances Haven't Closed NetSight's Gap

The problem NetSight targets is real but narrower than the proposal presents, and sonar technology has not made it obsolete. Sensor hardware has leapt ahead (Kongsberg's synthetic aperture sonar resolves roughly 13 mm), and several vendors now sell AI target recognition. Yet every product we found hands a candidate list to a human for confirmation, and none documents calibrated per-contact confidence or a probability-of-detection (Pd) map for civilian debris search. Deep Trekker's "Sonar Systems Explained" page is an operator's guide that calls sonar work "more of an art than science" and never mentions AI, probability of detection or statistical confidence, so it neither proves nor disproves NetSight's gap. NetSight's four novelty claims survive only in narrower wording: Pd and search-completeness maps are standard in search-and-rescue and naval mine clearance, and calibrated sonar uncertainty is already published for synthetic aperture sonar; the open slot is the combination, applied to civilian debris and ghost nets. The Indian facts are mostly usable but need edits: the 807-mile/54-debris/1-wreck survey is confirmed (but it was in Sri Lankan waters), the ~2% gear-loss figure is confirmed (but a Kerala study found far higher losses), the 37.9% figure could not be found (the published SDMRI figure is 43.17%), and Navy equipment is supported only by secondary sources. The weakest part is demand: in India, ghost nets are found by divers on shallow reefs, no sonar-based ghost-net programme or agency requirement turned up, and the "half of suspects are wrong" statistic comes from a German Baltic study. The verdict is to continue, but pitch NetSight as a trust-and-coverage layer with modest claims, not as the fix for a proven Indian crisis. Evidence tags used below: "opened" means a researcher fetched and read the page; "summary only" means the fact rests on a search-result snippet or a page that returned an error. Absence-of-evidence claims come from limited web searching and are not proof of absence.

## Deep Trekker's page teaches operators; it does not automate anything

The Deep Trekker article ("Sonar Systems Explained: Imaging, Side Scan, 3D & USBL", by Chad Gillen, dated 1 September 2026) is an educational explainer covering echosounders, CHIRP, Doppler logs, USBL positioning, side-scan, 2D imaging and 3D imaging ([Deep Trekker](https://www.deeptrekker.com/news/sonar-systems)). One caveat on method: our fetch tool returned a model-written summary rather than raw text, so the quotations below are as reported by that summary and the page itself was not checked byte-for-byte. The product lineup it describes is Blueprint Subsea Oculus multibeam imaging sonars (M370S, M750D, M1200D, M3000D, C550D), side-scan options for its ROVs, and a 3D imaging ecosystem with SLAM; Deep Trekker's support site confirms it resells the Oculus family and exports data for Blueprint's Viewpoint software ([Deep Trekker support](https://www.support.deeptrekker.com/accessories/sonars/supported-sonars)).

What the page says is overwhelmingly about human skill. Sonar use is described as "more of an art than science in many cases"; finding small objects "requires practice"; the operator picks altitude and angle, holds the ROV still to avoid smear, and follows a four-step manual routine (stabilise, 360-degree polar scan, align to zero bearing, switch to a ~90-degree sector scan); multibeam data "requires advanced data processing and specialized software" ([Deep Trekker](https://www.deeptrekker.com/news/sonar-systems)). It does concede real limits that support NetSight's premise: sonar "cannot distinguish between overlapping objects at the same slant range", wide shadows can hide neighbouring objects, and multibeam picks up marine life and particulates "requiring more filtering". Its search-and-recovery claims are modest: side-scan helps locate and guide divers, and ROV imaging sonar lets operators verify a find before sending people in. Its only automation is SoundTiles, which stitches sonar images into geo-referenced mosaics; that is mosaicking, not target detection.

What the page does not say matters more for NetSight. Our terminology check found no mention of AI, automatic target recognition or probability of detection, and "confidence" appears only as "operational confidence" (operators confirming their path during a dive). The word "debris" does not appear. "Coverage" is treated as geometry: the "10% rule" (hold altitude at 10% of range) yields "around 70% seabed coverage", described as one of the most reliable guidelines, which is an opinion, not a measurement, and says nothing about the chance of finding a given object inside the covered area ([Deep Trekker](https://www.deeptrekker.com/news/sonar-systems)). The linked resource articles were not fetched, so an AI mention elsewhere on the site is unchecked. The honest reading is that Deep Trekker is an ROV vendor selling imaging tools to human pilots; the page is consistent with "detection is still manual in that workflow" but cannot speak for the rest of the industry.

### Vendors have better sensors and assistive AI, with a human still signing off

The wider market is more advanced than that page suggests, mainly at the high end. Kongsberg's HISAS2020 synthetic aperture sonar, previously sold only inside HUGIN AUV packages, is now available standalone for AUVs, USVs, ROVs and towfish, with first trials giving roughly 13 mm resolution; the vendor says it helps operators "classify targets with greater confidence" but publishes no range, coverage or AI figures ([Kongsberg](https://www.kongsberg.com/news/news-archive/2026/validated-at-sea-hisas2020-delivers-on-its-promise/)). Sonardyne's Solstice multi-aperture sonar is fitted to mine-warfare AUVs, with target recognition living in separate MINTACS software rather than the sonar ([ADS Group](https://www.adsgroup.org.uk/knowledge/industry-member-news/sonardyne-s-solstice-mcm-sonar-now-mintacs-compatible-for-ran/)). So yes, the sensor is a step change over classic side-scan, but it sits on defence and survey-grade AUVs, while ROV-class tools such as Oculus remain live-imaging aids for a human.

Automatic target recognition (ATR) exists commercially, but as a shortlist generator. Kongsberg's SAS Target Assistant, built with Cathx Ocean, "automatically detects and measures objects of interest" and produces a target list "for review/confirmation"; no confidence scores, Pd or false-alarm rates are published ([Kongsberg](https://www.kongsberg.com/news/news-archive/2024/sas-target-assistant/)). Chesapeake's SonarWiz 8.1 advertises a CNN-based ATR ([Sea Technology](https://sea-technology.com/sonarwiz-8-1-0)), though a third-party profile calls it a prototype with no published accuracy, so its maturity is disputed. Charles River's AutoTRap on Teledyne Gavia reports a vendor-claimed 90% probability of detection for truncated conical objects on a rocky Icelandic seabed, with no false-alarm figure ([Ocean Science & Technology](https://www.oceansciencetechnology.com/feature/charles-river-analytics-maritime-autonomy-technologies/)). EvoLogics' Sonobot has onboard AI recognition (pre-trained classes plus user-trainable) tested by Dutch police, with no accuracy figures found; this rests on search snippets because the vendor pages errored ([EvoLogics](https://evologics.de/object-recognition)). Teledyne Gavia's 2022 "Object of Interest Search" mentions "man-made debris" as a query, though its current availability is unconfirmed ([iConnect007](https://iconnect007.com/article/130693/teledyne-marine-announces-sale-of-three-gavia-auvs-to-terradepth/130696/milaero)). In India itself, Goa Fire Services bought two AquaEye handheld AI sonars for drowning searches, with only vendor claims of effectiveness ([Herald Goa](https://www.heraldgoa.in/?p=458483)), and DRDO's NSTL is building AI side-scan mine recognition (summary only) ([The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/defence/2025/11/14/deep-sea-defence-upgrade-drdo-develops-ai-enabled-mine-hunter-for-indian-navy-what-can-it-do.html)). So "nobody has done AI sonar detection" is false, as the proposal's own section 13 already admits.

Is detection still manual? In civilian and ROV workflows, mostly yes. Chesapeake's February 2026 article describes today's status quo as processors "slogging through hundreds of contacts" and positions ML as a still-in-development remedy meant to "cut false alarm review by an order of magnitude"; it says detections will "carry probability estimates" but gives no calibration method or validation data ([Maritime Magazines](https://www.maritimemagazines.com/marine-technology/202602/the-future-of-sonar-processing)). Hypack Marine Search still has users mark targets by hand and offers a real-time mosaic to confirm 100% coverage, plus a "confidence value" derived from comparison with user-built examples ([Officer.com](https://www.officer.com/tactical/swat/rescue-equipment/product/12116559/hypack-marine-search)). FarSounder announced funding in January 2026 to add AI target recognition, so it is not yet shipping (headline only; page errored) ([Unmanned Systems Technology](https://www.unmannedsystemstechnology.com/2026/01/farsounder-secures-innovation-funding-to-integrate-ai-target-recognition-in-3d-sonar/)). The competitive implication is double-edged: the gap is real today, but vendors are visibly moving toward "probability" outputs, so a gap based on *undocumented* calibration will close faster than one based on *validated* calibration. NetSight's defensible edge is evidence (reliability diagrams, measured Pd), not the idea.

## Four novelty claims survive only in narrower wording

The research tested each claim against prior art. The pattern is the same throughout: the absolute versions are wrong, the "for civilian debris and ghost nets" versions hold up on current evidence.

| Claim as written | Verdict | Why | Suggested wording |
|---|---|---|---|
| Existing tools and SIH teams give no calibrated confidence | Holds for debris/net tools; overstated generally | GhostNetZero reports a single detection rate; Stolte 2022 was manual. But calibrated uncertainty is published for synthetic aperture sonar, and SeeByte ATR outputs a "mine-like" score (calibration undocumented) | "We found no debris or ghost-net tool that documents calibrated per-contact confidence." |
| No civilian debris tool reports a Pd/search-completeness map; it exists only in naval MCM | Half true | Nothing found for debris/nets, but Pd, sweep width and probability-of-success maps are standard in search-and-rescue and Bayesian wreck/aircraft search, and IHO survey standards require a "full sea floor search" | "No published civilian debris or ghost-net side-scan workflow reports a terrain-conditional Pd map." Drop "only in MCM." |
| No public side-scan dataset has labelled ghost nets | Consistent, not exhaustively checked | Every dataset we inspected has other classes; GhostNetZero's data are not public | "No public dataset we found contains labelled ghost nets." |
| GhostNetZero has no published calibration or coverage map | Consistent with the preprint landing page, not fully verified | The preprint says no data or code are available; neither the PDF nor the live platform was read by our researchers | "The preprint (v3) and press coverage report no calibration or coverage map." Check the PDF before asserting. |

On the first claim, the closest prior art is Orescanin et al. (2023), who built a variational-inference network for synthetic aperture sonar with calibrated uncertainty, reaching about 92% accuracy and gaining roughly 4 points by rejecting the most uncertain 10% of samples; the task was classifying imaging artifacts, not debris ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/uncertainty-quantification-with-deep-learning-through-variational/)). SeeByte's ATR gives operators a "how mine-like" measure but has no calibration documentation we could find ([SeeByte](https://seebyte.com/products/atr-system/)). A single search for conformal or calibrated side-scan object detection found nothing sonar-specific. The novelty is therefore application and validation, not technique.

The second claim is where a knowledgeable judge will push back. SeeByte's 2024 paper inserts simulated mine-like contacts into real side-scan to build a map of ATR performance by terrain, and its Neptune system sends the AUV back over low-performance areas ([arXiv 2404.18663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18663v1)); that is conceptually the same target-injection Pd study NetSight proposes. Gazagnaire et al. combine seabed models with ATR to produce Pd, false-alarm rate and percent coverage by seafloor class ([IOA](https://www.ioa.org.uk/system/files/proceedings/j_gazagnaire_p_beaujean_j_stack_combining_model-based_and_in_situ_performance_prediction_to_evaluate_detection_and_classification_performance.pdf)), and McMahon et al. (2017) plan AUV searches with per-environment detection and false-alarm models validated on real synthetic aperture data ([arXiv 1707.07662](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.07662)). Outside defence, the US Coast Guard's SAROPS builds probability maps and sweep-width tables ([Theory of Search](https://navcen.uscg.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/Theory_of_Search.pdf)). Note the catalog-record-only status of several MCM papers; their full text was not read. NetSight's proposal wording in section 13 ("we adapt the navy's proven Pd approach") is already close to right; the section 1 table's "every existing tool" phrasing is not.

The third and fourth claims are the strongest, with a caution. The AI4Shipwrecks benchmark (286 images, best IoU 0.445) shows how unsolved even public wreck detection is ([arXiv 2401.14546](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.14546v2)), and the Marine Debris forward-looking sonar dataset has ten classes but no nets and is not side-scan ([arXiv 2503.22880](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.22880v1)). GhostNetZero (Microsoft, WWF Germany, Accenture) is a non-peer-reviewed preprint reporting ~90% "detection rate" on Baltic and Puget Sound data, while press figures vary between 90, 94 and 95%; the landing page states "no data or code available" ([EcoEvoRxiv](https://ecoevorxiv.org/repository/view/10294/)). The proposal cites 0.82 to 0.94 centroid detection on 412 private images; our researchers did not see those specifics on the page they read, so the team should confirm them against the PDF. The GEOMAR/sonoware GhostNetBusters project (June 2024 to January 2026) reports no performance figures publicly ([GEOMAR](https://geomar.de/en/ghostnetbusters)); it is a project to watch because it targets exactly NetSight's niche with an open-source tool.

## India finds ghost nets with divers, and the proposal's facts need edits

The documented Indian practice is not sonar. In the Gulf of Mannar, the best-studied hotspot, the Suganthi Devadason Marine Research Institute (SDMRI) surveyed debris by roving diver and belt transect between February 2018 and March 2019 (summary only) ([FAO AGRIS](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/65de4b4c0f3e94b9e5cd1ed1)), and SDMRI's director says net removal from coral should be done only by scientist divers ([Mongabay-India](https://india.mongabay.com/2025/09/india-needs-a-marine-litter-policy-as-plastic-waste-chokes-corals/), opened). Elsewhere the response is ad hoc: a UNDP-backed Tamil Nadu project reports 16.2 t of debris removed, 12.9 t of it abandoned nets (summary only) ([UNDP India](https://www.undp.org/india/stories/protecting-marine-life-how-tamil-nadus-coastal-communities-are-clearing-abandoned-fishing-nets)); the Fishery Survey of India says it retrieved about 7 tonnes of ghost gear off the Andhra-Odisha coast over three years, and WWF-India's May 2023 trawler trials got only "small bits of discarded nets" because most gear settles on the seabed (summary only) ([ICSF](https://icsf.net/?p=107424)). A June 2026 Mongabay investigation, which was opened, says India has no systematic way to track, retrieve or recycle lost gear, that a Karnataka buy-back scheme exists, and that some incentive schemes were gamed by fishers staging retrievals ([Mongabay News](https://news.mongabay.com/2026/06/indias-fishers-confront-homegrown-ghost-gear-problem/)). It does not mention sonar. We found no evidence of sonar used for ghost-net or routine debris detection in India, and no NIOT, NCCR or Navy programme for it.

Where Indian sources do show side-scan on debris, it is hard-object work. After the MV X-Press Pearl sank off Colombo in 2021, INS Sarvekshak ran the survey at Sri Lanka's request ([CGI Hambantota](https://www.cgihambantota.gov.in/section/press-releases/indian-naval-ship-ins-sarvekshak-completes-800-miles-of-survey-around-mv-xpress-pearl/), opened). Planys, a Chennai ROV firm, describes a post-Cyclone Vardah (2016) side-scan survey of a south Indian jetty berth for suspected sunken wreckage, with results geo-tagged on a dashboard; the page does not say whether interpretation was manual ([Planys](https://planystech.com/cases/sonar-survey/), opened). Two inferences follow, and they are inferences, not findings. First, Gulf of Mannar reefs are shallow, diver-surveyed habitats where side-scan is of limited relevance; a sonar tool matters more for the soft-bottom trawl-ground gear that WWF-India's trials suggest is settling on the seabed. Second, the Indian bottleneck looks like "nobody surveys," not "surveyors cannot triage fast enough," which is a different problem from the one NetSight's central statistic describes. Notably, the proposal's example JSON uses coordinates near 9.28 N, 79.1 E, roughly the Gulf of Mannar and Palk Bay region, exactly where the documented detection method is diving.

Two related claims in the proposal are weaker than they look. It says NIOT, NHO, IWAI, the Coast Guard and port trusts "already run" side-scan surveys; we confirmed the Navy, NIOT's multibeam and synthetic aperture work on the IAF An-32 wreck ([ETV Bharat](https://www.etvbharat.com/english/national/bharat/niot-locates-iaf-an32-aircraft-debris-in-seabed-off-chennai-coast-seven-years-later/na20240112173650005005564)) and a commercial firm (Planys) serving ports, but found no IWAI or Coast Guard side-scan obstruction survey; IWAI is only described as using geospatial hydrographic surveys. And Navy survey ships are tasked for charting and defence, so the proposal should not imply they are available for civil debris search. The demand picture overall is "plausible, not shown": the underlying debris and gear problem is well documented, but we found no agency expressing or funding a sonar-based ghost-net search requirement. We have not seen the SIH26057 problem statement itself, which the proposal quotes, and an official MoES/NIOT problem statement is some evidence of institutional interest, though not of budget or a buyer.

## Sonar contacts keep needing a second sensor to settle them

The misleading-detection record supports NetSight's "trust" argument more strongly than its "all-clear" argument. In January 2024 Deep Sea Vision released a plane-shaped HUGIN 6000 AUV sonar image as a possible Amelia Earhart aircraft; in November 2024 the company reported follow-up imagery showing "a natural rock formation", about ten months later and by its own follow-up, with no independent review reported ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/image-believed-show-amelia-earharts-plane-was-rock-formation-not-crash-rcna181578)). The 2011 Baltic Sea Anomaly, a roughly 60 m circular "object", was attributed by Woods Hole's Hanumant Singh to distortion from a cheap, improperly calibrated instrument, with experts leaning toward a glacial feature (secondary source; no peer-reviewed study found) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltic_Sea_anomaly)). In the MH370 search, a cluster of small bright deep-tow sonar contacts reported in May 2015 turned out, after an AUV follow-up, to be an uncharted shipwreck at about 3,900 m ([gCaptain](https://gcaptain.com/shipwreck-found-in-search-for-missing-mh370-aircraft)). The 1987 Loch Ness Operation Deepscan contacts remain unexplained ([Loch Ness Project](https://lochnessproject.org/FIELDWORKGROUNDTRUTH/DEEPSCAN%20OP/DEEPSCAN%20OPERATION%20LOCH%20NESS.html)). None of these is a side-scan debris-clearance failure, but they share a pattern: sonar yields candidates, and a second sensor (camera, ROV, recovery) assigns identity.

The only hard false-alarm numbers come from the ghost-net study NetSight already uses. Stolte et al. (2022) interpreted side-scan manually over 5,820 ha (58.2 km²) of the German Baltic in 45 days and listed 549 suspect positions; divers verified 176, of which 94 were confirmed lost fishing gear ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmars.2022.981840)). Our arithmetic: 94 of 176 is 53%, which the paper reports as 52%; only 32% of suspects were checked at all, and 373 stayed unresolved. The authors blame natural edges, reefs and soft seabed whose reflectivity resembles a lost trawl net. This shapes how the proposal should word its headline: "even experts are right only about half the time" overstates it. The accurate statement is that about half of the suspects divers checked were real gear, the verified subset may be biased, and the study reports no miss rate, so recall is unknown. That last point actually strengthens NetSight's argument, since the field reports survey effort in hectares rather than probability of detection, but it needs to be said correctly. SeeByte's work adds that ATR performance on side-scan degrades rapidly in complex terrain ([arXiv 2404.18663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18663v1), abstract level), which is the physical case for a terrain-conditional Pd.

The "false all-clear" side is thinner. The cases we found are coverage and charting failures, not detection failures in covered ground. USS San Francisco (2005) and USS Connecticut (2021) struck uncharted seamounts, with the Navy citing navigation-procedure and risk-management errors and, for Connecticut, a route partly through unsurveyed areas ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_San_Francisco_(SSN-711)); [Pacific Fleet](https://www.cpf.navy.mil/newsroom/news/article/3040738/us-navy-releases-command-investigation-into-the-uss-connecticut-grounding/), summary only). A 2022 Geoscience Australia review of MH370 data covered only about 4,900 km², 29% of the 17,000 km² requested (summary only) ([ATSB](https://atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-02/mh370-data-review-2022-final-report-v2.pdf)), and an independent analyst group claims about 30.5 km² were never scanned (advocacy-leaning, unverified by the ATSB) ([Airline Ratings](https://www.airlineratings.com/articles/incomplete-atsb-mh370-review-creates-disbelief)). A plain coverage mosaic, which Hypack already offers, would expose unsurveyed gaps; NetSight's Pd model adds value only for the harder question of what was missed inside surveyed swaths. We found no documented case where a side-scan "channel clear" declaration was followed by a missed hazard, and the proposal should not imply there is one. The classic mechanisms in the proposal's section 2.4 (nadir gap, shadow ambiguity, range-dependent resolution, layback error) could not be sourced in this research pass and should be cited to IHO S-44 or a sonar textbook.

The Indian cases are instructive but are not side-scan debris cases. After the 16 July 2024 Shirur landslide in Karnataka, radar and Navy sonar signals led a state minister to confirm on 24 July that Arjun's truck had been detected in the Gangavali river ([The News Minute](https://www.thenewsminute.com/kerala/karnataka-landslide-kerala-man-arjuns-lorry-found-in-gangavali-river)); the Army reportedly said the large object could be the lorry or a mobile tower (summary only). Parts were pulled up in August, a September find of steering and clutch parts was disowned by the owner, and the truck was recovered on 25 September, 72 days after the landslide ([Onmanorama](https://www.onmanorama.com/news/kerala/2024/09/25/72-days-search-arjun-timeline-events-kozhikode-truck-body-gangavali.html)). We found no source saying which July signal was the truck, so "premature announcement" is the fair label, not "sonar false positive"; the case shows detections that could not be identified without physical recovery in a debris-laden, fast river. The positive Indian counterexample is NIOT locating IAF An-32 debris in January 2024 at about 3,400 m using multibeam, synthetic aperture sonar and photography, years after a surface search failed ([The Week](https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2024/01/12/over-seven-years-later-wreckage-of-missing-iaf-an-32-traced-in-bay-of-bengal.amp.html)); we cannot say the 2016 search covered that location, so this is not a missed detection. Dwarka, Hooghly, bridge-collapse and Kerala/Tamil Nadu wreck searches were not researched.

## Continue, but pitch trust and coverage rather than a crisis

The problem exists in three layers of different strength. That marine debris and ghost gear are serious in India is well documented and strong: a Kerala loss rate near 21%, nets at 43% of Gulf of Mannar reef debris, and an acknowledged absence of any national tracking system. That sonar-based detection is slow and unreliable is documented for the Baltic and by vendor statements, but has no Indian measurement behind it; this is moderate. That the technology has not already solved the trust-and-coverage problem is supported by every vendor page we read and by a limited prior-art search, but vendors are visibly moving toward probability outputs, and defence labs may hold unpublished tools; this is moderate and time-limited. That an Indian agency needs and will use such a tool is unevidenced. A fair summary for the team: the pain is real, the gap is probably real, the buyer is unproven, and the proposal's headline statistics lean on non-Indian data.

| What to change | Why |
|---|---|
| Replace "every existing tool, every SIH team" with "no debris or ghost-net tool we found documents calibrated confidence or a Pd map" | Absolute claims invite rebuttal; evidence is limited-search absence |
| Cite SeeByte (2024), Gazagnaire, McMahon (2017), Orescanin (2023), SAROPS as prior art, and describe NetSight as adaptation plus validation | A judge from the mine-countermeasures field will name these; citing them first reads as honesty |
| Fix or drop 37.9%; use 43.17 +/- 5.48% of reef debris unless the MARESSOL report supports 37.9% with a clear denominator | Cannot be traced to a verified source |
| Say Sarvekshak surveyed Sri Lankan waters, for Sri Lanka, as a Navy wreck/debris task | Avoids implying Indian civil precedent |
| Pair the 2% figure with the Kerala 21% (11.6% lost) result | Richardson's sample is not India-specific; the Indian data make the case stronger |
| Reword the "52%" statistic to "about half of the suspects divers checked (176 of 549) were real gear" and add that no miss rate was reported | Matches what Stolte actually reported |
| Do not list IWAI, the Coast Guard or port trusts as side-scan operators without a source; note Navy assets are not civil resources | Not found in the evidence |
| State plainly that Indian ghost-net detection today is diver-based on reefs and that NetSight targets soft-bottom and post-disaster survey data | Prevents a judge catching the mismatch first |
| Re-check GhostNetZero's 412-image and 0.82 to 0.94 figures against the preprint PDF | Our researchers could not confirm them |

The strongest next steps are empirical rather than rhetorical: obtain one labelled Indian survey sector from NIOT, publish a reliability diagram against a raw-score baseline, and compare synthetic-injection Pd with real cut-out Pd, since the proposal's own risk section already names that transfer as the central technical risk. Open gaps remain: no Indian primary source for equipment or demand; the full GhostNetZero PDF and live platform unread; SonarWiz, Kongsberg, Exail and Thales documentation unchecked; Mass DMF numeric side-scan detection rates for derelict pots (a closest-match civilian Pd effort) unobtained because the report returned HTTP 403; the proposal's other citations (BenthicDINO's 20.7% rock/ripple confusion, the 98% to 40% sim-to-real figure, the Stolte cost of EUR 140 per hectare) not re-verified here; the "13 tagged SIH repos" count not tested; and no 2026 peer-reviewed ghost-net side-scan papers were found, though search coverage of late 2026 was limited.

## Conclusion

The most useful change in understanding is that the proposal's strongest evidence and its strongest market are in different places. The evidence that triage is slow and error-prone is European (Stolte), and the evidence that tools lack calibration is a vendor-marketing review; the Indian evidence shows an under-surveyed, diver-led, policy-poor sector where the first missing thing is a survey programme, not a classifier. That makes NetSight's most credible role a quality-assurance layer for the surveys that do happen (Navy, NIOT, port ROV contractors, post-cyclone clearances), where it can state what was searched and how far to trust each contact, rather than a ghost-net finder for Indian fisheries.

The second takeaway is about timing. Vendors now advertise "probability estimates" and "confidence values" without calibration evidence, so a claim of novelty will not survive long, while a measured result will. If the team can show, even on public data and injected targets, a calibrated P(real) with a reliability diagram and a Pd map whose synthetic and real-target estimates agree, it has something the researched vendors and the GhostNetZero preprint do not publish. If it cannot, the project reduces to another YOLO dashboard. That test, not the sonar-has-advanced objection, is what decides whether to continue.
